\chapter{Conclusions}
\label{ch:conclusions}

This thesis investigated the hydraulic response of four excess-flow valve obturators for hydrogen refuelling applications and translated the resulting CFD data into an interactive design tool. The study considered a fixed obturator position at a reference state of $437\,\mathrm{bar}$ absolute and $233\,\mathrm{K}$, with hydrogen as the principal working fluid and nitrogen as a complementary comparison. The 24-case factorial campaign combined two hole counts, two hole diameters and three inlet velocities for each gas. OpenFOAM calculations, consistent post-processing and documented data reduction provided a common basis for comparing pressure loss and axial loading.

The principal result is the persistent ranking $A>C>B>D$ for both pressure loss and positive-$x$ force at every investigated inlet velocity. Smaller total passage areas increase acceleration through the obturator and the associated hydraulic resistance and loading. For hydrogen at $15\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, configuration A gives approximately $297.6\,\mathrm{kPa}$ and $50.09\,\mathrm{N}$, compared with $15.55\,\mathrm{kPa}$ and $1.53\,\mathrm{N}$ for D. The contour analysis supports this interpretation by showing the strongest passage acceleration and pressure contrasts in the more restrictive geometries. These results identify a design trade-off: a larger fluid-induced load may assist closure, but it also carries a greater pressure penalty during normal operation. Consequently, the largest force alone does not identify an optimum obturator.

Nitrogen produces substantially larger dimensional loads at equal inlet velocity, principally because its prescribed density is about 14.25 times that of hydrogen. After normalization, the pressure-loss and force coefficients are much closer and retain the same geometric ordering. Configuration B shows the largest departures, which must be considered alongside the appreciable terminal fluctuations in its hydrogen calculations. The comparison supports approximately quadratic response trends within the investigated conditions; it does not establish gas interchangeability. In particular, equal inlet velocity corresponds to different mass flows for the two gases.

The completed Streamlit application makes these results accessible through two inputs: gas and target mass flow. For each obturator, a quadratic least-squares fit converts the selected flow into an estimated axial force and pressure loss, while the original CFD points remain visible. The tool distinguishes the sampled interval from extrapolation and marks a geometry-dependent estimate of the local Mach $0.3$ boundary. At or above this boundary, design-load outputs are withheld and a further CFD study is required. This provides a practical connection between the simulation campaign and preliminary spring-load selection, while retaining the assumptions behind the numerical prediction.

The local Mach assessment is especially relevant to nitrogen. The largest saved-volume reference Mach number is approximately $0.107$ for hydrogen and $0.298$ for nitrogen, both for configuration A at the highest inlet velocity. The latter is close to the adopted screening threshold. The tool projects the largest observed velocity-amplification ratio for each gas and geometry, rather than treating the inlet Mach number as representative of every passage. This estimate is a screening criterion, not a validated operating limit, and remaining below it does not independently establish negligible density changes.

The conclusions are limited by the stationary, constant-property model and the available numerical evidence. Terminal-window averages characterize the recorded iterative histories; they are not experimental measurements or proof of mesh independence. Hydrogen B requires particular care because its force variability and fit mismatch are larger than those of the other configurations. The shorter nitrogen A calculation at $5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, near-wall resolution and the distinction between saved flow fields and averaged integral responses also remain relevant. A force at one fixed position cannot determine spring stiffness, closure time or a certified trip setting without preload, stroke, friction and the displacement-dependent fluid load.

Further development should therefore prioritize numerical refinement of the most sensitive cases, additional flow points near the intended operating threshold, and compressible calculations where indicated by the local-flow and pressure-change diagnostics. Simulations at several obturator positions, followed by coupled motion and experimental pressure-loss and trip tests, would connect the present loading estimates to actual valve operation. The contribution of this work is a traceable comparison of candidate geometries and an implemented tool for interrogating that comparison, providing a quantitative starting point for the subsequent mechanical design and validation of the EFV.
