\chapter{Numerical Model and CFD Setup}
\label{ch:numerical_setup}

This chapter describes the mathematical model, computational domain, boundary conditions, discretization and HPC workflow adopted for the excess flow valve simulations. Numerical configuration details are taken from the \texttt{SIM\_DoE\_2210} case files. The supplied archive contains the solver configuration of all 24 factor combinations and complete \texttt{postProcessing} histories for the twelve hydrogen cases. The recorded hydrogen output can be inspected for near-steady behavior, although solver residual histories, independent grid-sensitivity results and nitrogen post-processing data are not included. The numerical setup and the status of these checks are therefore reported separately.

\section{Governing Equations and Modeling Assumptions}
\label{sec:governing_equations}

The numerical model represents three-dimensional, steady, single-phase flow around a fixed obturator. The OpenFOAM 10 solver \texttt{simpleFoam} solves an incompressible, constant-density Reynolds-averaged Navier--Stokes (RANS) system \cite{openfoam_user,moukalled2016}. Neglecting body forces, the mass-conservation equation is
\begin{equation}
    \nabla\cdot\mathbf{U}=0,
    \label{eq:continuity}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{U}$ denotes mean velocity. A schematic eddy-viscosity representation of the mean momentum balance is
\begin{equation}
    \nabla\cdot(\mathbf{U}\otimes\mathbf{U})
    =-\nabla P+\nabla\cdot\left[(\nu+\nu_t)
    \left(\nabla\mathbf{U}+(\nabla\mathbf{U})^{T}\right)\right],
    \label{eq:momentum}
\end{equation}
where $P$ is the kinematic (modified) pressure, $\nu=\mu/\rho$ is the molecular kinematic viscosity and $\nu_t$ is the modeled turbulent eddy viscosity. Here the isotropic part of the modeled Reynolds stress is understood to be incorporated into the modified pressure; the equation summarizes the physical closure rather than reproducing every discrete term implemented by \texttt{simpleFoam} \cite{menter1994,openfoam_user}. The solved pressure field has dimensions of $\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-2}}$; conversion to a pressure difference in pascals requires multiplication by an appropriate density.

The archived \texttt{constant/momentumTransport} dictionary selects a RANS formulation with the $k$--$\omega$ Shear Stress Transport (SST) model \cite{menter1994}. Its near-wall $k$--$\omega$ behavior and outer-flow $k$--$\epsilon$ blending make it a candidate for flows featuring adverse pressure gradients and separation. This choice does not, by itself, guarantee accuracy. In particular, some hydrogen operating points have relatively low inlet Reynolds numbers, so turbulence-model and inlet-condition sensitivity should be assessed before drawing general conclusions.

The working-fluid kinematic viscosity is defined in \texttt{constant/physicalProperties} using the constant-viscosity model:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=C++,caption={Parameterized constant-viscosity entry used in the OpenFOAM setup.}]
viscosityModel constant;
nu [0 2 -1 0 0 0 0] NU_VALUE;
\end{lstlisting}
The template placeholder illustrated here is replaced for each gas as described in Chapter~\ref{ch:doe}. In the supplied completed case dictionaries, the active values are $\nu_{\mathrm{H}_2}=1.05\times10^{-4}$ and $\nu_{\mathrm{N}_2}=1.51\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$. They correspond to the reference-state properties used in this campaign, rather than the later hypothetical $437\,\mathrm{bar}$, $-40\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ operating state. The archived \texttt{SIM\_DoE\_2210} inlet data include the initial turbulence quantities $k=10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-2}}$ and $\omega=10\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$. Their physical basis and influence on the computed loads require verification; they are not asserted here to be universally representative of the actual upstream system.

\subsection*{Assessment of the incompressible approximation}
The common guideline $Ma\lesssim0.3$ refers to the \emph{local} Mach number, $Ma=|\mathbf{U}|/c$, rather than only the inlet value \cite{anderson2017}. Velocity increases within the obturator passages may therefore produce locally compressible flow even when the inlet velocity is moderate. For illustration, if all inlet flow passed through the nominal holes of obturator A, continuity would give an average passage velocity approximately nine times the inlet velocity; this estimate is not a computed maximum because other flow paths and contraction effects must be examined. The eventual validity assessment should include a local-velocity check against gas sound speed and, if necessary, comparison with a compressible formulation. Until then, the incompressible model is treated as a modeling assumption, not a verified property of every case.

\section{Computational Domain and Boundary Conditions}
\label{sec:boundary_conditions}

The fluid domain is bounded by five principal named surface patches: \texttt{inlet}, \texttt{outlet}, \texttt{wall1}, \texttt{wall2} and \texttt{wallObt}. These names describe boundary regions of a single computational domain, not five independent fluid domains. The stationary obturator is represented by the \texttt{wallObt} surface. The inlet and outlet are positioned away from the most restrictive region to reduce direct interference with the internal flow; independence from boundary placement still requires a numerical check.

In the velocity field, an included \texttt{initialConditions} dictionary specifies the inlet vector for each operating point. At the outlet, the \texttt{inletOutlet} velocity condition permits a zero-gradient outflow and prescribes a value where local backflow is detected. The outlet pressure is a zero \emph{reference kinematic pressure}, not a specification that the system operates at zero absolute pressure. The $k$, $\omega$ and turbulent-viscosity fields require their own inlet, outlet and wall conditions, implemented through the corresponding OpenFOAM dictionaries; these should be documented together with their sensitivity when the setup is finalized.

\section{Numerical Discretization and Execution Workflow}
\label{sec:workflow_automation}

OpenFOAM applies a finite-volume discretization of the steady equations. In the \texttt{SIM\_DoE\_2210} \texttt{fvSchemes} dictionary, velocity convection uses a bounded \texttt{linearUpwindV} scheme with a limited velocity gradient, while the transport of $k$ and $\omega$ uses bounded upwind schemes. Gradient interpolation is mainly linear, and Laplacian terms employ a corrected formulation. These choices balance resolution and numerical robustness but do not eliminate the need to inspect oscillations or sensitivity to mesh quality.

Pressure--velocity coupling is based on the SIMPLE algorithm \cite{openfoam_user,moukalled2016}. The archived \texttt{fvSolution} configuration specifies ten non-orthogonal correction passes and equation relaxation factors of $0.9$ for velocity and $0.5$ for both turbulence variables. The \texttt{controlDict} specifies \texttt{endTime 3000} with \texttt{deltaT 1}; because this is a steady-state solver, the values $0$--$3000$ are iteration indices rather than physical seconds. Solver tolerances control the solution of individual algebraic systems; they must not be confused with a demonstrated convergence of the overall nonlinear flow solution.

Case execution is managed using Bash scripts that prepare the mesh, partition the domain and launch parallel meshing and solving on ARIES. The principal sequence is \texttt{blockMesh}, \texttt{surfaceFeatures}, \texttt{decomposePar}, parallel \texttt{snappyHexMesh} and parallel \texttt{simpleFoam}. Solver-only restarts are available without repeating the mesh stage. The generation of the complete factorial case set and subsequent retrieval of reduced outputs are addressed in Chapter~\ref{ch:doe}.

\section{Hybrid Mesh Generation Strategy}
\label{sec:mesh_generation}

The \texttt{SIM\_DoE\_2210} setup uses a structured hexahedral background grid generated by \texttt{blockMesh}, followed by local castellated refinement, surface snapping and layer generation using \texttt{snappyHexMesh} \cite{openfoam_user}. The background box extends from $(-0.010,-0.0145,-0.0145)\,\mathrm{m}$ to $(0.175,0.0145,0.0145)\,\mathrm{m}$. The dictionary specifies $370\times58\times58$ cells, giving $1{,}244{,}680$ \emph{background} cells with nominal spacing $\Delta x=\Delta y=\Delta z=0.5\,\mathrm{mm}$. This is not the final snapped-mesh cell count.

The geometry consists of imported STL surfaces named \texttt{inlet.stl}, \texttt{outlet.stl}, \texttt{wall1.stl}, \texttt{wall2.stl} and \texttt{wallObt.stl}. The last is replaced to generate the different obturator variants. Representative surface statistics for the obturator-A CAD files are provided in Table~\ref{tab:stl_stats}; these counts must not be assumed to be identical for all four obturators.

Feature edges are extracted using \texttt{surfaceFeatures}. The archived \texttt{snappyHexMeshDict} specifies surface refinement level~1 for the inlet and outlet and level~2 for the two wall surfaces and the obturator. It also defines a cylindrical local refinement region near the obturator, with endpoints $(0.035,0,0)\,\mathrm{m}$ and $(0.046,0,0)\,\mathrm{m}$, radius $0.011\,\mathrm{m}$ and refinement level~2. These are requested refinement settings; the final realized cell distribution must be checked from the generated mesh.

The \texttt{addLayers} option is enabled, with two surface layers requested on \texttt{wallObt} and an expansion ratio of~2. Layer survival and local near-wall resolution must be assessed using the completed mesh and $y^+$ values before asserting that the chosen SST wall treatment is adequately resolved. Recommended additions for the final chapter are a close-up of the cells around the holes, \texttt{checkMesh} diagnostics and a three-level mesh-sensitivity comparison for pressure drop and obturator force.

\section{HPC Infrastructure and Domain Decomposition}
\label{sec:hpc_environment}

The cases are prepared for execution on the ARIES High-Performance Computing (HPC) infrastructure at the University of Modena and Reggio Emilia \cite{hpc_aries}. SLURM manages job allocation; the reference setup requests one node and 52 MPI tasks. This is an allocation of MPI ranks and should not be equated with 52 physical cores without confirming the node hardware. The \texttt{scotch} domain decomposition method is specified to distribute cells over the subdomains while limiting communication interfaces \cite{pellegrini1996}.

The number of cells per subdomain and the processor-interface counts depend on the actual mesh and decomposition. Earlier load-balance statistics should therefore not be reused as performance evidence for the \texttt{SIM\_DoE\_2210} meshes. Final documentation should report the actual decomposition statistics, wall-clock runtime and resource use for representative accepted cases, if those logs are available.

\section{Numerical Verification and Current Limitations}
\label{sec:numerical_verification}

Numerical acceptance requires more than reaching the configured final iteration. The following independent checks are required before the simulation data can be used for validated engineering recommendations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item residual decay or stabilization together with a stable inlet--outlet pressure difference and axial force history;
    \item conservation of volume or mass flux between inlet and outlet and the absence of nonphysical field values;
    \item mesh quality, near-wall $y^+$ and a grid-sensitivity assessment of the principal integral responses;
    \item sensitivity to turbulence boundary conditions and assessment of maximum \emph{local} Mach number in the narrow passages.
\end{itemize}

The newly supplied hydrogen output comprises twelve complete histories recorded over $0$--$3000$ iterations, with pressure and force written at each iteration and $y^+$ data every 100 iterations. The final 301 iterations ($2700$--$3000$) provide a reproducible \emph{preliminary stability window}. For these hydrogen cases, the coefficient of variation of the total axial-force history in that interval ranges from approximately $0.33\%$ to $3.58\%$, while that of the pressure-difference history ranges from approximately $0.16\%$ to $2.25\%$. These are descriptive measures of terminal fluctuation, not certified convergence tolerances. Their derivation and the resulting preliminary values are provided in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}.

The current archive does not include the full solver residual histories, an outlet-flux function-object output, a completed grid-independence study or nitrogen post-processing time series. As a result, a final convergence and validation claim cannot yet be made for the complete $24$-case DoE.

\subsection*{Density convention in the force calculation}
The \texttt{forces} function object uses \texttt{rho rhoInf} to dimensionalize the loads calculated from the incompressible solution. The active \texttt{rhoInf} values in all supplied case dictionaries are $0.0838\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ for hydrogen and $1.165\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ for nitrogen. Thus, the force values stored in the new hydrogen \texttt{forces.dat} files are already reported in newtons using the hydrogen reference density; they must \emph{not} be multiplied by density again. The prior configuration value of $33.801\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ is not used in this newly supplied dataset.

The outlet kinematic pressure is prescribed as zero reference in \texttt{0/p}; this does not independently impose an absolute operating pressure. The choice of reference density and viscosity represents an assumed thermodynamic state. Predictions at $437\,\mathrm{bar}$ and $-40\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ would require a separately configured set of real-gas properties and a fresh appraisal of the incompressible approximation.
