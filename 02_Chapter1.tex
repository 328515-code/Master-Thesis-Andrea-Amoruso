\chapter{Numerical Model and CFD Setup}
\label{ch:numerical_setup}

This chapter describes the mathematical model, computational domain, boundary conditions, discretization and HPC workflow adopted for the excess flow valve CFD simulations. The purpose is to document the computational choices and their physical meaning in enough detail for an engineering reader to reproduce the workflow, using a model which represents a component of the hydrogen refuelling, not the complete dispensing installation (Chapter~\ref{ch:efv_operation}).

\section{Governing equations and their modeling}
\label{sec:governing_equations}

The numerical model represents three dimensional, steady, single-phase flow around a fixed obturator. The OpenFOAM 10 solver \texttt{simpleFoam} solves an incompressible, constant density Reynolds-averaged Navier--Stokes (RANS) system \cite{openfoam_user,moukalled2016}. \\
Under the assumed constant-density, steady-flow conditions and neglecting body forces, the continuity equation is
\begin{equation}
    \nabla\cdot\mathbf{U}=0,
    \label{eq:continuity}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{U}$ is the Reynolds-averaged velocity. A schematic eddy-viscosity representation of the mean momentum balance is
\begin{equation}
    \nabla\cdot(\mathbf{U}\otimes\mathbf{U})
    =-\nabla P+\nabla\cdot\left[(\nu+\nu_t)
    \left(\nabla\mathbf{U}+(\nabla\mathbf{U})^{T}\right)\right],
    \label{eq:momentum}
\end{equation}
where $P$ is the kinematic pressure, $\nu=\mu/\rho$ is the molecular kinematic viscosity and $\nu_t$ is the turbulent eddy viscosity. The solved pressure field has dimensions of $\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-2}}$; conversion to a pressure difference in pascals requires multiplication by an appropriate density.

The selected RANS turbulet model is the $k$--$\omega$ Shear Stress Transport (SST) \cite{menter1994}. 

The SST closure blends $k$--$\omega$ behavior near walls with an outer-flow $k$--$\epsilon$ formulation; it was selected to provide a RANS description of adverse pressure gradients and separation that will be present for sure in the problem object of the study, with a reasonable computational cost \cite{menter1994}.

The working-fluid kinematic viscosity is defined using the constant viscosity model:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=C++,caption={Parameterized constant-viscosity entry used in the OpenFOAM setup.}, captionpos=b]
viscosityModel constant;
nu [0 2 -1 0 0 0 0] NU_VALUE;
\end{lstlisting}

The template placeholder illustrated here is replaced for each gas as described in Chapter~\ref{ch:doe}. The original ambient-reference cases used $\nu_{\mathrm{H}_2}=1.05\times10^{-4}\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$ and $\nu_{\mathrm{N}_2}=1.51\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$. For the final high-pressure and low-temperature simulations, these entries are replaced by the gas-specific values $\nu_{\mathrm{H}_2}=2.709\times10^{-7}\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$ and $\nu_{\mathrm{N}_2}=7.932\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$. These are the active fluid-property settings verified in the final submitted case dictionaries. The inlet turbulence fields are initialized with the initial turbulence quantities $k=10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-2}}$ and $\omega=10\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$. These quantities are numerical inlet conditions; a physical justification requires knowledge of the upstream turbulence state. Sensitivity checks, rather than an unsupported claim of universality, are used to assess their influence on the integrated responses.

\subsection{Assessment of the incompressible approximation}
The common guideline $Ma\lesssim0.3$ refers to the \emph{local} Mach number, $Ma=|\mathbf{U}|/c$, rather than only the inlet value \cite{anderson2017}. Velocity increases within the obturator passages may therefore lead to local compressibility effects even when the imposed inlet velocity is moderate. For illustration, if all inlet flow passed through the nominal holes of obturator A, continuity would give an average passage velocity approximately nine times the inlet velocity; this estimate is not a computed maximum because other flow paths and contraction effects must be examined. The eventual validity assessment should include a local-velocity check against gas sound speed. The incompressible approximation is an explicit scope condition of the present model and is assessed through the characteristic velocities and pressure variations of the investigated geometries.

\section{Thermodynamic Reference State for the Hydrogen Dispenser}
\label{sec:cfd_highpressure_state}

The component considered in this work is intended for installation downstream of the pre-cooler and near the hydrogen dispenser (Section~\ref{sec:efv_reference_state}). The chosen application-oriented design condition is nominally $437\,\mathrm{bar}$ absolute and $233\,\mathrm{K}$ (approximately $-40\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$); it represents a specified operating point of a pressurized, pre-cooled delivery line, not the entire time-dependent fuelling history. Pre-cooling and the permitted delivery-temperature range are relevant to station design and the fueling procedure, as discussed in the SAE~J2601 protocol and the literature \cite{sae_j2601,elgowainy2017}.

In a constant-density solver, the effect of this state enters through the evaluated fluid properties, rather than through an imposed uniform absolute pressure offset. For the gas under consideration, the input density $\rho_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and dynamic viscosity $\mu_{\mathrm{ref}}$ are evaluated at the same pressure and temperature and are converted to $\nu_{\mathrm{ref}}=\mu_{\mathrm{ref}}/\rho_{\mathrm{ref}}$ before the flow calculation. Reference fluid databases such as NIST REFPROP provide the thermodynamic and transport quantities required at the selected state \cite{nist_refprop_online}. A separate numerical case must use its corresponding $\nu_{\mathrm{ref}}$; changing only \texttt{rhoInf} would rescale forces without incorporating a Reynolds-number change.

\begin{equation}
Re_{\mathrm{in}}=\frac{U_{\mathrm{in}}D_{\mathrm{in}}}{\nu_{\mathrm{ref}}},
\qquad
Ma_{\mathrm{local}}=\frac{|\mathbf{U}|}{c(p,T)},
\label{eq:highpressure_re_mach}
\end{equation}
where the local sound speed $c$ depends on the thermodynamic state. At high pressure, the absolute pressure itself does not automatically invalidate an incompressible model; the decisive questions concern the change of density across the valve and local flow acceleration. However, a rupture case with a large downstream pressure reduction can introduce strong compressibility, choked passages or other effects that a steady incompressible model cannot predict reliably \cite{anderson2017,white2016}.

Accordingly, the present fixed-obturator, velocity-controlled simulations are interpreted as hydraulic comparisons at specified reference states. A complete evaluation of a station rupture sequence, mechanical obturator motion or a large pressure-ratio discharge would require additional physical modeling, potentially involving a compressible transient solver and mechanical coupling. This distinction is central to applying the CFD results appropriately in hydrogen refuelling engineering.

\section{Computational Domain and Boundary Conditions}
\label{sec:boundary_conditions}

The fluid domain is bounded by five principal named surface patches: \texttt{inlet}, \texttt{outlet}, \texttt{wall1}, \texttt{wall2} and \texttt{wallObt}. These names describe boundary regions of a single computational domain, not five independent fluid domains. The stationary obturator is represented by the \texttt{wallObt} surface. The inlet and outlet planes are separated from the most restrictive obturator region to reduce artificial interactions between the imposed boundary conditions and the developing internal flow. The adequacy of those distances is a numerical modeling consideration, not a statement of a physically prescribed station layout.

\subsection{Meaning of the Boundary Conditions}
\label{sec:boundary_physical_interpretation}

The inlet condition fixes the axial velocity rather than prescribing a mass-flow rate at an independently specified operating pressure. For the constant-density model, a given inlet velocity corresponds to a volume flow proportional to the inlet area. The reference outlet kinematic pressure establishes the datum for the pressure field: the pressure \emph{difference} is the relevant output, while its dimensional value is obtained by multiplying the kinematic difference by the chosen density.

All three wall patches use the no-slip velocity condition. For the fixed obturator considered here, that choice means the solid remains stationary relative to the fluid; it is not a dynamic-mesh model of the valve closing. At the outlet, the backflow-capable velocity condition is useful for handling local recirculation near the boundary but does not replace verification of the outlet's distance from the disturbed region. These distinctions are relevant when transferring the numerical results to hydrogen refuelling applications because the full station pressure history and the component-specific thermal state are not directly imposed by this local steady solver \cite{openfoam_user,sae_j2601}.

\section{Numerical Discretization and Execution Workflow}
\label{sec:workflow_automation}

OpenFOAM applies a finite-volume discretization of the steady equations. In the \texttt{fvSchemes} dictionary, velocity convection uses a bounded \texttt{linearUpwindV} scheme with a limited velocity gradient, while the transport of $k$ and $\omega$ uses \texttt{bounded upwind} schemes. Gradient interpolation is mainly linear, and Laplacian terms employ a corrected formulation. This combination is intended to limit nonphysical oscillations while retaining useful resolution of the flow field. Its performance is evaluated using residual behavior and monitored integral quantities rather than assuming convergence from the discretization scheme alone.

Pressure--velocity coupling is based on the SIMPLE algorithm \cite{openfoam_user,moukalled2016}. The \texttt{fvSolution} configuration specifies ten non-orthogonal correction passes and equation relaxation factors of $0.9$ for velocity and $0.5$ for both turbulence variables. The \texttt{controlDict} specifies \texttt{endTime 3000} and \texttt{deltaT 1}. Since \texttt{simpleFoam} solves a steady problem, these values set the solver's iteration sequence rather than a physical transient lasting $3000\,\mathrm{s}$. An iteration limit is a computational stopping setting; convergence is assessed using residuals and stabilized engineering outputs.

Case execution is managed using Bash scripts that prepare the mesh, partition the domain and launch parallel meshing and solving on ARIES. The principal sequence is \texttt{blockMesh}, \texttt{surfaceFeatures}, \texttt{decomposePar}, parallel \texttt{snappyHexMesh} and parallel \texttt{simpleFoam}. Solver-only restarts are available without repeating the mesh stage. The generation of the complete factorial case set and subsequent retrieval of reduced outputs are addressed in Chapter~\ref{ch:doe}.


\subsection{Traceable Pre-processing and Solver Sequence}
\label{sec:preprocessing_sequence}

Each design point is treated as a self-contained computational case. Starting from a clean template avoids the accumulation of old mesh or field values when changing the obturator geometry. The geometry-specific \texttt{wallObt.stl} is copied into the corresponding case, and the fluid viscosity and inlet velocity are assigned before meshing. The processor decomposition is configured to match the SLURM task allocation, so that all subsequent mesh-generation and solution steps are consistent with the parallel execution environment.

The geometric refinement is concentrated around the obturator because this is where the narrow passages and strong gradients are expected. The meshing procedure has three distinguishable phases: castellated refinement, projection of boundary faces onto the imported geometry, and optional boundary-layer cell extrusion. These phases should not be conflated with the initial structured mesh count. The final computational grid contains a different distribution and number of cells than the \texttt{blockMesh} background grid \cite{openfoam_user}.

For an industrial handover, the essential reproducibility information consists of the CAD revision, the gas-property state, the active solver and meshing dictionaries, the processor allocation and the monitored response definitions. Keeping these elements together with each case allows changes in the geometry or setup to be traced to changes in the numerical outputs.

\section{Hybrid Mesh Generation Strategy}
\label{sec:mesh_generation}

The computational setup uses a structured hexahedral background grid generated by \texttt{blockMesh}, followed by local castellated refinement, surface snapping and layer generation using \texttt{snappyHexMesh} \cite{openfoam_user}. \\
The initial box extends from $(-0.010,-0.0145,-0.0145)\,\mathrm{m}$ to $(0.175,0.0145,0.0145)\,\mathrm{m}$, with $370\times58\times58$ cells, giving $1{,}244{,}680$ \emph{background} cells with nominal spacing $\Delta x=\Delta y=\Delta z=0.5\,\mathrm{mm}$. This is not the final snapped-mesh cell count.

The CAD definition is imported as triangulated STL surfaces named \texttt{inlet.stl}, \texttt{outlet.stl}, \texttt{wall1.stl}, \texttt{wall2.stl} and \texttt{wallObt.stl}, where the last one is replaced to generate the different obturator variants. The \texttt{snappyHexMeshDict} specifies surface refinement level~1 for the inlet and outlet and level~2 for the two wall surfaces and the obturator. It also defines a cylindrical local refinement region near the obturator, with endpoints $(0.035,0,0)\,\mathrm{m}$ and $(0.046,0,0)\,\mathrm{m}$, radius $0.011\,\mathrm{m}$ and refinement level~2. These are requested refinement settings; the final realized cell distribution must be checked from the generated mesh.

The \texttt{addLayers} option is enabled with an expansion ratio of~2. Two prismatic layers are requested on the obturator surface. The specified expansion ratio is~2. Realized layer coverage and near-wall resolution are evaluated from the generated mesh and wall-distance diagnostic, since requested layers do not necessarily survive snapping and mesh-quality constraints.

\section{HPC Infrastructure and Domain Decomposition}
\label{sec:hpc_environment}

The cases are prepared for execution on the ARIES High-Performance Computing (HPC) infrastructure provided by the University of Modena and Reggio Emilia \cite{hpc_aries}. SLURM manages the resource allocation; the case script requests one node with 52 MPI tasks. This is an allocation of MPI ranks and should not be equated with 52 physical cores. The \texttt{scotch} domain decomposition method is specified to distribute cells over the subdomains while limiting communication interfaces \cite{pellegrini1996}.

The number of cells per subdomain and the processor-interface counts depend on the actual mesh and decomposition.

\section{Numerical Verification and Current Limitations}
\label{sec:numerical_verification}

Numerical quality is evaluated independently of the configured stopping iteration. The following independent checks will be pursued before the simulation data can be considered valid for the final design tool:
\begin{itemize}
    \item residual decay or stabilization together with a stable inlet--outlet pressure difference and axial force history;
    \item conservation of volume or mass flux between inlet and outlet and the absence of nonphysical field values;
    \item mesh quality, near-wall $y^+$ and a grid-sensitivity assessment of the principal integral responses;
    \item sensitivity to turbulence boundary conditions and assessment of maximum \emph{local} Mach number in the narrow passages.
\end{itemize}

Chapter~\ref{ch:results} details how the principal post-processing quantities are extracted, averaged and compared across the selected cases.


\subsection{Interpretation of the Monitored Quantities}

Residual values indicate progress in the algebraic solution of the discretized equations, whereas the integrated pressure difference and obturator force indicate stability of the engineering outputs. Both types of evidence are useful. For the latter, a clearly defined terminal averaging interval makes the comparison reproducible. A mesh study should compare the sensitivity of $\Delta p$ and $F_x$ to refinement at a representative geometry and operating condition; the difference between two meshes is an estimate of numerical sensitivity, not necessarily an experimental error bound \cite{moukalled2016}.

The dimensionless near-wall distance $y^+$ is interpreted together with the turbulence-model wall treatment, the actual layer count and local mesh distortion. Likewise, the incompressibility check should be performed on locally accelerated flow, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:governing_equations}; inlet Mach number alone is not sufficient to characterize the region around the obturator holes \cite{anderson2017}. These checks establish the numerical context of a result before it is used in a design comparison.

\subsection*{Density convention in the force calculation}
The \texttt{forces} function object uses \texttt{rho rhoInf} to dimensionalize the loads calculated from the incompressible solution. The final high-pressure case dictionaries prescribe $\rho_{\mathrm{H}_2}=33.77\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ and $\rho_{\mathrm{N}_2}=481.352\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ through the active \texttt{rhoInf} entries. The older ambient-reference configurations use their own, much smaller density values. Accordingly, the output force vectors are already dimensionalized in newtons by the force function object. Multiplying these vectors by gas density again would produce an incorrect result.

The outlet kinematic pressure is prescribed as zero reference in \texttt{0/p}; this does not independently impose an absolute operating pressure. The choice of reference density and viscosity represents an assumed thermodynamic state.
