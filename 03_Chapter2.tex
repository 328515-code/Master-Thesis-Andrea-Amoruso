\chapter{Design of Experiments (DoE) $\And$ HPC Pipeline}
\label{ch:doe}

This chapter explains how the Design of Experiments (DoE) is constructed and translated into a reproducible set of OpenFOAM cases. The study is organized around four factors and 24 prescribed combinations. Particular attention is given to why these factors are relevant for a spring-loaded EFV, how geometry and fluid properties are handled, and how the computed outputs are elaborated. The resulting procedure supports repeatable comparisons among the obturator concepts introduced in Chapter~\ref{ch:efv_operation}.

\section{Definition of the DoE Parameter Space}
\label{sec:doe_parameters}

The purpose of the DoE is first to compare EFV geometry and hydraulic loading for \emph{hydrogen}, the designated fuel to use during refueling. Nitrogen was added as a secondary working fluid because nitrogen is widely used for inerting and purging gaseous-hydrogen systems in industrial practice \cite{h2tools_purging,doe_hydrogen_practices}. This does not imply that nitrogen reproduces the thermodynamic or hydraulic behavior of hydrogen since the two gases have different density and viscosity, making the analysis useful only for complementary CFD comparisons. Hydrogen results retain priority in the engineering interpretation and implementation.

The four physical parameters are working fluid, inlet velocity, number of obturator holes and diameter of each hole. Their selected levels are listed in Table~\ref{tab:doe_factors}. The resulting full factorial design comprises
\begin{equation}
    N_{\mathrm{cases}}=2_{\mathrm{fluids}}\times3_{\mathrm{velocities}}\times
    2_{\mathrm{hole\ counts}}\times2_{\mathrm{diameters}}=24.
    \label{eq:doe_cases}
\end{equation}
Treating hole count and diameter as separate factors allows their individual effects and interaction to be investigated. For example, doubling hole diameter increases the nominal area of each circular passage by a factor of four, whereas doubling the hole count doubles the total passage area, provided the hole diameters are unchanged. Their effects on force and pressure difference are nevertheless not necessarily independent because the detailed flow structure changes with both parameters \cite{white2016,crane410}.

The incompressible momentum solution depends on kinematic viscosity, and thus on the Reynolds number $Re=U_{\mathrm{in}}D_{\mathrm{in}}/\nu$. At equal inlet velocity and geometry, a comparison of the two gases changes this Reynolds number. Dimensional pressure losses and forces also depend on density, so a cross-fluid comparison must distinguish the kinematic flow response from its dimensional scaling. The atmospheric reference state is a modeling convention: the CFD case configurations use the high-pressure reference properties documented in Table~\ref{tab:fluid_properties_highp}.
The gas property set, is the only parameter that defines the reference state approximation, since the \texttt{0/p} outlet reference value is relative and not absolute as said in Chapter \ref{ch:numerical_setup}. The distinction is important because both Reynolds number and dimensional force scaling depend on the chosen state \cite{sae_j2601,nist_refprop}.

\subsection{High-Pressure, Low-Temperature reference}
\label{sec:doe_highpressure_state}

The industrial EFV is situated in the hydrogen delivery line \emph{after the pre-cooling heat exchanger and close to the dispenser}. This selected location makes a cold, high-pressure gas state a more representative physical reference than ambient conditions. The final application-oriented CFD state is $p_{\mathrm{ref}}=437\,\mathrm{bar}$ and $T_{\mathrm{ref}}=233\,\mathrm{K}$ (approximately $-40\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$); its choice and the connection with H35/H70 dispensing and T40 fuel-delivery conditions are explained in Section~\ref{sec:efv_reference_state} \cite{sae_j2601,elgowainy2017}.

At the high-pressure reference state, density and viscosity must be calculated consistently at the same $(p,T)$, from a real fluid property model. For the OpenFOAM implementation, the required quantities are:
\begin{equation}
\rho_{\mathrm{ref}}=\rho(p_{\mathrm{ref}},T_{\mathrm{ref}}),\quad
\mu_{\mathrm{ref}}=\mu(p_{\mathrm{ref}},T_{\mathrm{ref}}),\quad
\nu_{\mathrm{ref}}=\frac{\mu_{\mathrm{ref}}}{\rho_{\mathrm{ref}}}.
\label{eq:highpressure_properties}
\end{equation}
For an incompressible \texttt{simpleFoam} case, $\nu_{\mathrm{ref}}$ enters the governing flow equations through the \texttt{physicalProperties} dictionary imposing the fluid characteristics, whereas $\rho_{\mathrm{ref}}$ enters the dimensional load calculation through the \texttt{forces} function object's \texttt{rhoInf} parameter used so only for the post processing calculations.\\

Because the comparison here is velocity-controlled, the volumetric flow rate at a given inlet area is approximately $Q=U_{\mathrm{in}}A_{\mathrm{in}}$, while the mass flow rate is $\dot m=\rho_{\mathrm{ref}}Q$. Comparisons intended to use a fixed dispenser mass flow demand should instead match $\dot m$ and adjust the inlet velocity for the selected density.

The ambient reference data in Table~\ref{tab:fluid_properties} was useful as a separate baseline for sensitivity analysis. Their densities, viscosities and dimensional forces are not transferred unchanged to the $437\,\mathrm{bar}$, $233\,\mathrm{K}$ cases. Any extension to strongly nonuniform pressure and temperature fields would require reconsideration of the constant-density approximation.

\subsection{Inlet Velocity Levels and Reference Flow Rates}
\label{sec:inlet velocities}
The three inlet velocities selected for both the Hydrogen and Nitrogen cases are $5$, $10$ and $15\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. These levels provide three distinct flow-demand conditions with a common reference geometry, enabling sensitivity to velocity to be assessed without changing the valve geometry. They are numerical factors rather than direct assignments to particular SAE~J2601 refuelling event, since such assignment would require the station flow requirement and a valve spring force specification \cite{sae_j2601}. 

The nominal circular inlet diameter is $D_{\mathrm{in}}=7.8\,\mathrm{mm}$, giving
\begin{equation}
    A_{\mathrm{in}}=\pi\left(\frac{D_{\mathrm{in}}}{2}\right)^2
    =\pi(0.0039\,\mathrm{m})^2\simeq4.778\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m^2}.
    \label{eq:inlet_area}
\end{equation}
For a prescribed uniform inlet velocity, the corresponding nominal volumetric and reference mass-flow rates are
\begin{equation}
    Q_{\mathrm{nominal}}=U_{\mathrm{in}}A_{\mathrm{in}},
    \qquad
    \dot m_{\mathrm{ref}}=\rho_{\mathrm{ref}}Q_{\mathrm{nominal}}.
    \label{eq:inlet_flow}
\end{equation}
The quantities in Table~\ref{tab:flow_regimes} use the nominal CAD inlet area and reference densities. The latter is used then to check the simulated flux.

Likewise, the inlet Mach number is insufficient to certify incompressibility within the more restrictive passages Section~\ref{sec:governing_equations}), that will be checked in the next chapter.

\subsection{Obturator Geometries (\texttt{wallObt})}
Two levels of hole count and two levels of hole diameter define the four CAD variants. With the nominal total hole area
\begin{equation}
    A_{\mathrm{holes}}=n\pi\frac{d^2}{4},
    \qquad
    \beta=\frac{A_{\mathrm{holes}}}{A_{\mathrm{in}}},
    \label{eq:open_area_ratio}
\end{equation}
the open-area ratio $\beta$ quantifies the nominal restriction relative to the inlet. It represents the sum of the specified circular hole areas and is not necessarily the complete effective flow area if other bypass passages exist.

The area ratio $\beta$ is useful for preliminary geometric comparison, but it is not by itself a loss or force coefficient: local contraction, the obturator shape and downstream recirculation also influence the pressure distribution and integral loading \cite{white2016,crane410}. For an EFV installed in a refuelling line, the objective is to compare the compromise between hydraulic resistance during normal filling and fluid induced actuation load. Neither the smallest nor the largest opening should be declared optimal without the associated mechanical requirements.


\section{Factorial comparison and interaction analysis}
\label{sec:factorial_analysis}

The experimental design may be viewed as a set of twelve gas--velocity conditions, each evaluated on the four combinations of hole number and diameter. This arrangement permits paired geometric comparisons at fixed gas and inlet velocity. For example, the difference $R(n=6,d)-R(n=3,d)$ estimates the influence of hole count at the chosen diameter, while $R(n,d=3.0\,\mathrm{mm})-R(n,d=1.5\,\mathrm{mm})$ estimates the effect of hole size. Here $R$ can represent pressure loss, axial force or a dimensionless response coefficient.

A hole-count--diameter interaction occurs when the effect of increasing hole count depends on which diameter is selected. A convenient two-level interaction contrast for each gas and velocity is
\begin{equation}
I_{nd}=
\left[R(6,3.0)-R(3,3.0)\right]-
\left[R(6,1.5)-R(3,1.5)\right],
\label{eq:hole_interaction}
\end{equation}
where diameters are expressed in millimetres for brevity.

A nonzero contrast will indicate that the two geometrical effects are not additive on the scale of the selected response. \\

The three velocity levels can also be used to examine the departure from a simple proportional or quadratic dependence on inlet speed.

The factorial approach makes the design comparison systematic, but the chosen simulation points do not constitute independent experimental replicates. The engineering conclusions should be connected to numerical checks, clear force sign conventions and the mechanical closing-force criterion introduced in Section~\ref{sec:efv_principle}.

\section{Automated case lifecycle and traceability}
\label{sec:case_lifecycle}

The automation process is organized into a sequence of controlled transformations:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Template definition}: establish a common computational domain, solver settings, patch names and processing function objects.
\item \textbf{Parameter injection}: select the fluid kinematic viscosity, reference density and inlet velocity; replace the obturator STL using the corresponding geometry.
\item \textbf{Case preparation}: generate or refine the mesh and write the domain decomposition for the allocated MPI task count.
\item \textbf{Execution}: submit the case to SLURM and execute the OpenFOAM workflow on the allocated node.
\item \textbf{Output extraction}: collect the pressure, inlet-flow, wall-distance and force histories, retaining each case identifier.
\item \textbf{Data reduction}: use the same averaging interval and physical reference quantities for each comparable output.
\end{enumerate}
The sequence has been designed to reduce manual editing errors and making it possible to compare computational cases generated under equivalent numerical settings. The CFD dictionaries are the authoritative description of each case, while the Python and Bash scripts automate their repeated application.\\

For refueling system design, this traceability is particularly valuable because a change in operating pressure or temperature is not merely a label change: the associated density and viscosity may change, affecting Reynolds number and dimensional force scaling. A change in the pressure application therefore requires a deliberately specified thermodynamic state, rather than applying a post-processing multiplier to a result obtained with an unrelated kinematic viscosity \cite{nist_refprop,white2016}.

\section{Python case generation and submission}
\label{sec:python_doe_generator}

A Python-based workflow is used to reduce repetitive manual case preparation. Its intended operations are to copy a reference OpenFOAM case, insert the selected viscosity and inlet velocity, replace the \texttt{wallObt.stl} geometry and assign a unique directory name to each factor combination. Cases can then be transferred to the HPC system and submitted with the relevant SLURM execution script.

An illustrative generator, updated to the factor levels and gas properties adopted in this study, is reproduced in Appendix~\ref{sec:app_doe_gen}. As the original execution script was not available for verification, the listing is presented as an illustrative, reproducible implementation rather than as a verbatim record of the submitted jobs.

A complete case record associates the selected gas and its viscosity and density settings with the inlet velocity, obturator identifier, solver settings and mesh dictionaries. Using a consistent naming convention makes automated post-processing reliable, but the scientific interpretation is independent of the physical directory location. The simulation settings and output definitions provide the provenance necessary for an engineering comparison.

\section{Automated Remote Post-Processing Extraction}
\label{sec:scp_extraction}

The OpenFOAM run directories include processor meshes and field files that are substantially larger than the reduced \texttt{postProcessing/} outputs used for preliminary response screening. A local Python script can retrieve each case \texttt{postProcessing/} directory using \texttt{scp} while preserving the simulation's directory identifier, thereby reducing unnecessary transfer and the risk of mixing cases.

The example in Appendix~\ref{sec:app_scp_script} uses configurable paths, to retrieve, the output files associated with their case factors before any pressure or force statistics are computed.

\section{Response variables and planned DoE analysis}
\label{sec:doe_analysis}

The principal outputs from the simulations are the inlet to outlet pressure difference, the total axial force on the stationary obturator and the inlet flow rate. 
For an accepted steady solution, one may define:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta p=\rho_{\mathrm{ref}}\left(P_{\mathrm{in}}-P_{\mathrm{out}}\right),
    \qquad
    F_x=F_{p,x}+F_{\nu,x},
    \label{eq:doe_responses}
\end{equation}
where $P$ is kinematic pressure averaged on the surface, $\rho$ is the fluid density, $F_{p,x}$ is the pressure force axial component acting on the obturator and $F_{\nu,x}$ is the viscous force axial component acting on the obturator. In post processing file \texttt{forces.dat}, $F_x$ has already been dimensionalized with \texttt{rhoInf} (OpenFOAM computes the forces normalized by density). The sign of $F_x$ is defined relative to the positive streamwise $x$ axis.\\

For comparisons across flow levels and gases, the following coefficients are obtained:
\begin{equation}
    P_{loss}=\frac{\Delta p}{\tfrac12\rho_{\mathrm{ref}}U_{\mathrm{in}}^2},
    \qquad
    C_{F,x}=\frac{F_x}{\tfrac12\rho_{\mathrm{ref}}U_{\mathrm{in}}^2 A_{\mathrm{in}}}.
    \label{eq:response_coefficients}
\end{equation}
Here $A_{\mathrm{in}}$ is the selected reference area that must be used consistently in all cases.

Because these are deterministic numerical runs without independent physical replicates, an apparent statistical fit must not be interpreted as experimental uncertainty.
