\chapter{Results and Post-Processing}
\label{ch:results}

This chapter presents the integral fluid dynamic results of the final high pressure $437\,\mathrm{bar}$, low temperature $233\,\mathrm{K}$ (approximately $-40\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$) simulations. As the target application is an excess-flow valve (EFV) for hydrogen-vehicle refuelling, \emph{hydrogen is the primary working fluid}. Nitrogen is investigated as a supplementary industrial gas and its numerical response is discussed after the hydrogen findings. 

All the data shown in tables, plots and contours below are computed OpenFOAM responses, not experimental measurements.

\section{Structure of OpenFOAM Post-Processing Data}
\label{sec:postprocessing_structure}

Each calculation writes five sets of numerical outputs: area-averaged kinematic pressure at the inlet and outlet, integrated inlet velocity, pressure and viscous loads on the obturator, and wall-distance data. The corresponding function-object locations are \texttt{inlet\_pressure\_avg}, \texttt{outlet\_pressure\_avg}, \texttt{inlet\_volume\_flux}, \texttt{forceCoeffs\_Obt} and \texttt{yPlus1}. Although the force output folder includes the term force coefficients, the active OpenFOAM function computes an actual force, not a coefficient.

The solved pressure field is \emph{kinematic} pressure, expressed in $\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-2}}$. The dimensional pressure loss and axial force component are evaluated using
\begin{equation}
\Delta p=\rho_{\mathrm{ref}}\left(P_{\mathrm{in}}-P_{\mathrm{out}}\right),
\qquad F_x=F_{p,x}+F_{\nu,x},
\label{eq:processed_responses}
\end{equation}
the same equations introduced at Section \ref{sec:doe_analysis}. \\

The global positive-$x$ force is distinct from a mechanically verified closing-direction force.

The submitted dictionaries for the high pressure low temperature give $\rho_{\mathrm{H}_2}=33.77\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, $\nu_{\mathrm{H}_2}=2.709\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$, $\rho_{\mathrm{N}_2}=481.352\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ and $\nu_{\mathrm{N}_2}=7.932\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$, respectively for each gas we got density and viscosity used by the incompressible solver as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:doe_highpressure_state}.

\section{Data reduction and terminal numerical behavior}
\label{sec:plotly_dashboard}

The \texttt{simpleFoam} output index, representing solver iteration and not elapsed physical time, indicates to have pressure and force histories extending to iteration~3000. To apply a reproducible comparison, the last 301 samples of each case were extracted: indices 2700--3000. For a numerical response $R_i$, the arithmetic mean and the coefficient of variation are
\begin{equation}
\overline{R}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}R_i,
\qquad
\mathrm{CV}(R)=
\frac{\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(R_i-\overline{R})^2}}
{|\overline{R}|}\times100\%,\qquad N=301.
\label{eq:terminal_average}
\end{equation}
This statistic measures variation within the selected terminal interval. Reaching the last iteration or observing a small CV does not independently prove residual convergence, mesh independence, or experimental validation \cite{moukalled2016,openfoam_user}.

\section{Near wall diagnostics and local Mach check}
\label{sec:near_wall_verification}

The final post-processing outputs include minimum, maximum and average wall-distance values for the selected patches. At iteration 3000, the maximum $y^+$ reported on the obturator ranges from approximately 156 to 1256 across the hydrogen cases. The nitrogen cases exhibit larger maxima, exceeding 4000 in the most restrictive and highest-velocity combination, this occurs due to the lower viscosity knowing that the formulation for the $y^+$ is:

\begin{equation}
y^+=\frac{u_\tau \ y}{\nu}
\label{eq:y+}
\end{equation}\\

Where $y$ is the distance of the first layer cell centroid from the wall, $u_\tau$ is the friction velocity (a function of the wall stresses) and $\nu$ is the kinematic viscoity.

The reported maxima are compatible with the use of wall functions, but maxima alone do not demonstrate that every wall cell satisfies the intended $y^+$ range \cite{menter1994,openfoam_user}. Terminal variations of integral responses must also be distinguished from solver-residual convergence and mesh independence. The greatest hydrogen terminal variation occurs for obturator B at the higher velocities. 

The inlet Mach number alone does not describe the acceleration through the obturator channels. The local Mach number is
\begin{equation}
M(\mathbf{x})=\frac{|\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})|}{a(\mathbf{x})},
\qquad
a^2=\left(\frac{\partial p_{\mathrm{abs}}}{\partial\rho}\right)_s,
\label{eq:local_mach_results}
\end{equation}
where $a$ is the isentropic speed of sound and $p_{\mathrm{abs}}$ is thermodynamic absolute pressure, not the kinematic gauge-pressure field solved by \texttt{simpleFoam} \cite{anderson2017}. Because the present model does not solve an energy equation or a compressible equation of state, the following diagnostic uses a fixed reference sound speed:
\begin{equation}
a_{\mathrm{ref,H}_2}=1611.8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}},
\qquad M_{\mathrm{ref}}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{|\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})|}{a_{\mathrm{ref,H}_2}}.
\label{eq:hydrogen_sound_speed}
\end{equation}
This value was evaluated at $T=233\,\mathrm{K}$ and $p_{\mathrm{abs}}=43.7\,\mathrm{MPa}$ using the normal-hydrogen Helmholtz equation of state of Leachman et al., implemented in CoolProp 8.0.0.\footnote{J. W. Leachman et al., \emph{Fundamental Equations of State for Parahydrogen, Normal Hydrogen, and Orthohydrogen}, J. Phys. Chem. Ref. Data 38, 721--748 (2009), \url{https://doi.org/10.1063/1.3160306}. Implementation: \url{https://coolprop.org/fluid_properties/fluids/Hydrogen.html}.} The same evaluation gives $\rho=33.790\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, close to the prescribed CFD value of $33.77\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$; the CFD density is retained in all pressure and force calculations.

For comparison, the calorically perfect ideal-gas estimate is $a_{\mathrm{ideal}}=\sqrt{\gamma R_{\mathrm{H}_2}T}=1159.9\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, using $\gamma=1.4$ and $R_{\mathrm{H}_2}=4124.5\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$. This ideal-gas value is not substituted for the real-gas sound speed at the high-pressure reference state.

Table~\ref{tab:hydrogen_local_mach} distinguishes two measures at solver iteration 3000. $U_{\mathrm{ch,slice}}$ is the largest interpolated speed on the two longitudinal planes $y=0$ and $z=0$, restricted to the obturator region $0.035\leq x\leq0.046\,\mathrm{m}$. It samples the channel passages and their immediate exits but is not a maximum over every channel. $U_{\max,\mathrm{3D}}$ is obtained independently by scanning the original cell-centred velocity field over every processor subdomain. The latter supplies a domain-wide bound on the resolved cell-centred channel velocities and avoids treating a two-dimensional slice as a complete three-dimensional check.

\subsection{Velocity and pressure contours through the obturator}
\label{sec:hydrogen_contours}

Figures~\ref{fig:h2_velocity_contours} and~\ref{fig:h2_pressure_contours} compare the four obturators at $U_{\mathrm{in}}=15\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, where the geometric differences are most visible. They show the longitudinal plane $y=0$ at iteration 3000, with flow from left to right along the positive $x$ direction. The camera, spatial scale and colour limits are identical for all configurations. Velocity magnitude uses the Turbo map; pressure uses Coolwarm. The ranges, $0$--$140.98\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ and $-224.23$--$299.35\,\mathrm{kPa}$, cover both longitudinal planes for all twelve hydrogen cases. The neutral Coolwarm colour denotes the midpoint of this range, not zero pressure.

The displayed pressure is $p_g=\rho_{\mathrm{H}_2}P$, relative to the zero-pressure outlet. It is neither the absolute operating pressure nor a pointwise pressure-loss coefficient. Negative values therefore indicate pressure below the outlet reference, not negative absolute pressure. White areas are outside the cut fluid domain, including solid regions. Point values are interpolated from the cell-centred solution before slicing.

\begingroup\emergencystretch=1em
The narrow passages of A produce a concentrated high-speed jet and the strongest upstream-to-downstream pressure contrast. Configuration C, which has twice as many small holes, shows lower peak speeds and a more distributed acceleration pattern on this plane. B and D have larger holes and weaker velocity amplification and pressure contrasts on the common scale. The integral pressure-loss and axial-force ranking is also $A>C>B>D$. However, one section does not intersect every hole or capture all off-plane velocity maxima. In particular, Table~\ref{tab:hydrogen_local_mach} shows why the modest slice velocities of D must not be interpreted as its full-domain peak velocity.
\par\endgroup

Downstream of the passages, high-speed fluid enters a wider region and is surrounded by lower-speed fluid. This is consistent with jet development and mixing after the restriction. Velocity-magnitude contours alone cannot establish the direction of recirculation or identify vortices; those interpretations require vector, streamline or vorticity information. Likewise, a local static-pressure minimum is not the area-averaged inlet-to-outlet pressure loss used in Tables~\ref{tab:hydrogen_pressure} and~\ref{tab:hydrogen_force}.

These final-iteration fields complement, rather than replace, the 301-sample integral averages. The elevated terminal variation of B at $15\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ means that its displayed pattern should not be interpreted as a uniquely converged steady structure. ParaView also reports non-manifold triangulation warnings for the source meshes; small local contour omissions cannot be excluded. The contours are used for qualitative interpretation, while the full-domain Mach scan reads the original volume velocities independently of the slicing operation.

\section{Nitrogen: Secondary Industrial-Gas Comparison}
\label{sec:nitrogen_comparison}

Hydrogen is the actual refueling gas and retains priority in the design interpretation. Nitrogen was included as a second working fluid because it is a familiar industrial gas in hydrogen-system purging and inerting procedures; its inclusion broadens the physical-property comparison without making it a substitute for hydrogen in fuel delivery \cite{h2tools_purging,doe_hydrogen_practices}. Nitrogen and hydrogen differ substantially in density, viscosity and resulting Reynolds number at the same imposed velocity.

The nitrogen results are reported in Tables~\ref{tab:nitrogen_pressure} and~\ref{tab:nitrogen_force}. The corrected numerical output for obturator A at $5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ has been independently checked against the hydrogen data and no longer contains the previously duplicated histories. Its pressure-loss and force coefficients of variation over this final 301-sample interval are approximately $0.759\%$ and $0.723\%$, respectively. The shorter numerical history is retained as a transparent qualification, not interpreted as proof of convergence.

\section{Dimensionless Comparison of Hydrogen and Nitrogen}
\label{sec:results_operating_states}
\label{sec:gas_coefficient_comparison}

To separate the dominant density and velocity scaling from the geometric response, the two gases are compared using the coefficients defined in Section~\ref{sec:doe_analysis}, Eq.~\eqref{eq:response_coefficients}:
\begin{equation}
\overline{P}_{loss}=\frac{\overline{\Delta p}}{\tfrac12\rho_{\mathrm{ref}}U_{\mathrm{in}}^2},
\qquad
\overline{C}_{F,x}=\frac{\overline{F}_x}{\tfrac12\rho_{\mathrm{ref}}U_{\mathrm{in}}^2A_{\mathrm{in}}}.
\label{eq:mean_response_coefficients}
\end{equation}
For consistency with Eq.~\eqref{eq:inlet_area}, the force coefficient uses the nominal inlet area $A_{\mathrm{in}}=\pi(0.0039)^2=4.7783624\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m^2}$ for every case, rather than the slightly smaller discretized patch area used to report actual flow rates. The density is that of each gas's active dictionary. Because the denominators are fixed within a case, normalizing the terminal mean is equivalent to averaging the normalized samples. The calculations use the unrounded response values retained in the repository's audited response table; displayed coefficients are rounded to two decimals.

The dimensional nitrogen responses are roughly fourteen times the hydrogen responses, but the normalized values are much closer: $\rho_{\mathrm{N}_2}/\rho_{\mathrm{H}_2}\simeq14.25$. Both tables retain the ordering $A>C>B>D$ for both gases at every inlet velocity. For A, C and D, paired gas differences are below approximately 2.5\% in both coefficients. This near-collapse indicates that the leading dimensional difference is density scaling within the present constant-property models. It does not imply identical thermodynamics or complete dynamic similarity.

Obturator A has $\overline{P}_{loss}\simeq78$--$79$ and $\overline{C}_{F,x}\simeq275$--$278$, whereas D has approximately $4.07$--$4.14$ and $8.19$--$8.56$. Their weak velocity dependence is consistent with an approximately quadratic dependence of pressure loss and force on inlet speed. The large numerical force coefficients reflect normalization by the small inlet area; they are not efficiencies or mechanical closing-force ratios.

Configuration B exhibits the largest differences. Its hydrogen pressure coefficient rises from 8.16 at $5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ to 9.29 at $10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, while nitrogen stays near 8.6--8.7. At $15\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, the nitrogen pressure and force coefficients are approximately 6.0\% and 8.1\% below the hydrogen values. These departures should be considered alongside B's hydrogen terminal CV values of 10.25\% for pressure and 12.46\% for force at that velocity. Since normalization divides by a constant, it does not reduce the relative terminal variation; the observed gas difference cannot be attributed uniquely to a physical gas effect from these data alone.

At equal inlet velocity and geometry, the Reynolds-number ratio is $Re_{\mathrm{N}_2}/Re_{\mathrm{H}_2}=\nu_{\mathrm{H}_2}/\nu_{\mathrm{N}_2}\simeq3.42$. Residual coefficient differences can therefore involve Reynolds-number effects as well as mesh, wall-treatment and iterative effects. The comparison also remains at approximately equal volumetric flow, not equal mass flow. Moreover, the largest nitrogen pressure loss is about 9.7\% of the nominal absolute reference pressure, compared with about 0.68\% for hydrogen; a successful hydrogen low-Mach check does not independently validate nitrogen's constant-density approximation.

These coefficients support relative hydraulic screening of the stationary geometries. They do not convert the recorded positive-$x$ force into a verified closing force, establish a trip threshold, or describe the transient refuelling protocol. Ambient-reference results and the present cold high-pressure results must remain separate: rescaling density alone would omit the changes in viscosity and flow structure. Hydrogen remains the primary design fluid, with nitrogen providing a supplementary comparison of normalized response.

\section{Implications for Excess-Flow Valve Design}
\label{sec:results_efv_implications}

The principal hydrogen result is a consistent trade-off: smaller effective passages increase both hydraulic resistance and positive-$x$ obturator loading. Therefore the configuration yielding the greatest force is not automatically the best valve, because the pressure-loss requirement during normal flow must also be met. An engineering choice requires the permissible loss, mechanical force balance, motion direction, spring properties and expected excess-flow conditions (Section~\ref{sec:efv_principle}).

The nitrogen study supports the comparison of fluid-property effects but does not change the hydrogen-first objective of the work. The tabulated CFD responses provide the numerical foundation for a design comparison tool, which is described in Chapter~\ref{ch:selection_tool}. Implementing and testing that software is a separate final development activity, not an additional round of flow simulations.
