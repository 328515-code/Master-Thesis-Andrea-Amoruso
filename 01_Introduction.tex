\chapter*{Introduction}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Introduction}
\markboth{Introduction}{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

The development of hydrogen refuelling infrastructure requires components capable of controlling gaseous hydrogen under demanding operating conditions while providing reliable protection against abnormal release. A refuelling station integrates gas supply, compression, storage, temperature conditioning and dispensing equipment. In this environment, the valve system contributes both to routine regulation and to the mitigation of faults involving unwanted gas flow, with the general requirements for these installations addressed by specific standards.

An Excess-Flow Valve is a device designed to automatically shut off or limit gas flow when a specified flow condition is reached \cite{iso19880_3}. In a representative spring-loaded construction, fluid-dynamic forces on a movable obturator compete with the restoring spring force. Normal service requires the valve to remain open with an acceptable pressure drop; abnormal flow may produce a load sufficient to move the obturator towards its seat. This operating principle creates an engineering trade-off: greater restriction can increase the available hydrodynamic force, but it can also increase pressure losses during normal dispensing \cite{white2016,crane410,honeywell_efv}.

The application to vehicle refueling, using SAE~J2601 as reference protocols, influences the delivery temperature, flow rate, and pressure-ramp behavior togetehr with fuel delivery at approximately $-40\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ \cite{sae_j2601}. Such standards establish the operating context, but they do not eliminate the need for component-specific hydraulic and mechanical analysis.

In the industrial scheme studied in this work, the EFV is installed \emph{downstream of the pre-cooler, near the dispenser}, where it is exposed to high-pressure, pre-cooled hydrogen. The station architecture and the role of pre-cooling are discussed in ISO~19880-1 and in the technical literature on hydrogen dispensing \cite{iso19880_1,elgowainy2017,nrel_csd2014}. The valve's required safety performance falls within the scope of ISO~19880-3 \cite{iso19880_3}. \\

For this project, the final application-oriented CFD campaign uses a nominal reference state of $437\,\mathrm{bar}$ and $233\,\mathrm{K}$ (approximately $-40\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$). This is close to the initially considered $430\,\mathrm{bar}$ operating point and is interpreted as an absolute-pressure reference state for gas-property evaluation. 

Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) provides detailed information about the flow field inside complex passages, including local acceleration, separation, pressure distribution and viscous loading, as well as making it possible to obtain integral quantities, such as the pressure difference between inlet and outlet and the net force on an obturator, from the computed velocity and pressure fields \cite{moukalled2016}.

The objective of this thesis work, is to develop a reproducible numerical procedure for assessing the fluid-dynamic behaviour of four EFV obturator geometries. The research combines a three-dimensional OpenFOAM 10 model with a full-factorial Design of Experiments (DoE), batch execution on the ARIES high-performance computing (HPC) infrastructure and systematic post-processing \cite{openfoam_user,hpc_aries}. The investigated design factors are the working gas (Hydrogen or Nitrogen), inlet velocity ($5$, $10$ and $15\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$), obturator hole count (three or six) and individual hole diameter ($1.5$ or $3.0\,\mathrm{mm}$). Their combinations define 24 numerical cases. The research will be deliberately centred on hydrogen, because hydrogen is the fuel delivered by the intended refuelling installation. Nitrogen is a supplementary comparison gas, relevant to the broader industrial handling of hydrogen systems, including inert-gas purging and pressure checking; its different physical properties help expose the influence of fluid selection on the numerical responses \cite{h2tools_purging,doe_hydrogen_practices}.

The final simulation campaign covers the complete 24-case factorial and its pressure loss and hydrodynamic force outputs are analysed and then elaborated for the generation of a tool, which aims to help engineers in choosing the right valve for the required application parameters.\\

The methodology is organised around three engineering questions. First, how do hole count and diameter affect the pressure drop and axial hydrodynamic force on a fixed obturator? Second, how do these responses depend on inlet velocity and the working fluid? Third, how can the resulting CFD information be prepared in a consistent form for future comparison and selection of valve concepts? The numerical responses include dimensional pressure differences and forces, together with dimensionless loss and force coefficients. The operating state comparison requires density and viscosity to be taken consistently at the chosen pressure and temperature using the real-fluid property models \cite{nist_refprop_online}.

An essential modeling distinction is that the present calculations solve steady incompressible flow around a \emph{fixed} obturator, not the complete mechanical trip event or the timed refuelling process. Moreover, acceleration through restricted passages can increase the local Mach number even when the inlet flow is relatively slow; this motivates checking the applicability of the incompressible approximation \cite{anderson2017}.\\
