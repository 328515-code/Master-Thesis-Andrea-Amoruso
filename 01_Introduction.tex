\chapter*{Introduction}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Introduction}
\markboth{Introduction}{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

The development of hydrogen refuelling infrastructure requires components capable of controlling gaseous hydrogen under demanding operating conditions while providing reliable protection against abnormal release. A refuelling station integrates gas supply, compression, storage, temperature conditioning and dispensing equipment. In this environment, the valve system contributes both to routine regulation and to the mitigation of faults involving unwanted gas flow. The general requirements for these installations are addressed by ISO~19880-1, while ISO~19880-3 specifically covers safety-performance requirements for high-pressure valves, including excess-flow valves (EFVs) \cite{iso19880_1,iso19880_3}.

An EFV is a device designed to automatically shut off or limit gas flow when a specified excess-flow condition is reached \cite{iso19880_3}. In a representative spring-loaded construction, fluid-dynamic forces on a movable obturator compete with the restoring spring force. Normal service requires the valve to remain open with an acceptable pressure drop; abnormal flow may produce a load sufficient to move the obturator towards its seat. This operating principle creates an engineering trade-off: greater restriction can increase the available hydrodynamic force, but it can also increase pressure losses during normal dispensing \cite{white2016,crane410,honeywell_efv}. A successful design must therefore account for both flow resistance and actuation behaviour.

The application to vehicle refuelling adds further constraints. SAE~J2601 defines protocols for H35 and H70 gaseous hydrogen vehicle filling and identifies the influence of delivery temperature, flow rate and pressure-ramp behaviour. The T40 class represents fuel delivery at approximately $-40\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ \cite{sae_j2601}. Such standards establish the operating context, but they do not eliminate the need for component-specific hydraulic and mechanical analysis. In particular, a steady computational model of a stationary obturator is not equivalent to a transient simulation of valve closure.

In the industrial arrangement studied here, the EFV is installed \emph{downstream of the pre-cooler, near the dispenser}, where it is exposed to high-pressure, pre-cooled hydrogen rather than ambient gas. The station architecture and the role of pre-cooling are discussed in ISO~19880-1 and in the technical literature on hydrogen dispensing \cite{iso19880_1,elgowainy2017,nrel_csd2014}; the valve's required safety performance falls within the scope of ISO~19880-3 \cite{iso19880_3}. For this project, the final application-oriented CFD campaign uses a nominal reference state of $437\,\mathrm{bar}$ and $233\,\mathrm{K}$ (approximately $-40\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$). This is close to the initially considered $430\,\mathrm{bar}$ operating point and is interpreted as an absolute-pressure reference state for gas-property evaluation. The preceding ambient-reference calculations remain a separate baseline.

Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) provides detailed information about the flow field inside complex passages, including local acceleration, separation, pressure distribution and viscous loading. The finite-volume method makes it possible to obtain integral quantities, such as the pressure difference between inlet and outlet and the net force on an obturator, from the computed velocity and pressure fields \cite{moukalled2016}. For engineering purposes, these values must be interpreted together with the chosen fluid properties, boundary conditions and numerical assumptions.

The objective of this thesis, entitled \emph{Computational Fluid Dynamics for the design of Excess-Flow valves in Hydrogen refuelling systems}, is to develop a reproducible numerical procedure for assessing the fluid-dynamic behaviour of four EFV obturator geometries. The research combines a three-dimensional OpenFOAM 10 model with an automated full-factorial Design of Experiments (DoE), batch execution on the ARIES high-performance computing infrastructure and systematic post-processing \cite{openfoam_user,hpc_aries}. The investigated design factors are the working gas (hydrogen or nitrogen), inlet velocity ($5$, $10$ and $15\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$), obturator hole count (three or six) and individual hole diameter ($1.5$ or $3.0\,\mathrm{mm}$). Their combinations define 24 numerical cases. The research is deliberately centred on hydrogen, because hydrogen is the fuel delivered by the intended refuelling installation. Nitrogen is a supplementary comparison gas, relevant to the broader industrial handling of hydrogen systems, including inert-gas purging and pressure checking; its different physical properties help expose the influence of fluid selection on the numerical responses \cite{h2tools_purging,doe_hydrogen_practices}.

The final simulation campaign covers the complete 24-case factorial matrix with case-specific high-pressure density and viscosity settings. Its pressure-loss and hydrodynamic-force outputs are analysed principally for hydrogen, followed by secondary nitrogen comparisons. The resulting CFD dataset is intended to underpin an engineering design tool; that software is described conceptually in this thesis, while its final implementation is reserved for the concluding development stage.

The methodology is organised around three engineering questions. First, how do hole count and diameter affect the pressure drop and axial hydrodynamic force on a fixed obturator? Second, how do these responses depend on inlet velocity and the working fluid? Third, how can the resulting CFD information be prepared in a consistent form for future comparison and selection of valve concepts? The numerical responses include dimensional pressure differences and forces, together with dimensionless loss and force coefficients. The operating-state comparison requires density and viscosity to be taken consistently at the chosen pressure and temperature; real-fluid property models are particularly important for pressurized hydrogen \cite{nist_refprop_online}.

An essential modeling distinction is that the present calculations solve steady incompressible flow around a \emph{fixed} obturator. They describe the hydraulic loading for the prescribed geometry, not the complete mechanical trip event or the timed refuelling process. Moreover, acceleration through restricted passages can increase the local Mach number even when the inlet flow is relatively slow; this motivates checking the applicability of the incompressible approximation \cite{anderson2017}. The model remains useful for comparative design provided its assumptions are stated and its outputs are interpreted within their scope.

The thesis is structured as follows. Chapter~\ref{ch:efv_operation} introduces EFV working principles and their function within a hydrogen refuelling installation. Chapter~\ref{ch:numerical_setup} describes the computational geometry, physical model, mesh, solver and HPC execution procedure. Chapter~\ref{ch:doe} explains the factorial parameters and the automation process used to prepare the simulations. Chapter~\ref{ch:results} develops the post-processing strategy and discusses the computed hydraulic responses. Chapter~\ref{ch:selection_tool} describes the architecture of the interactive comparison tool, and Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusions} summarises the engineering findings and directions for further development.
