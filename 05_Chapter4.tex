\chapter{Interactive selection tool development}
\label{ch:selection_tool}

The CFD results are useful to a valve designer only if they can be related to an engineering requirement. For a spring-loaded excess-flow valve, one such requirement is the mass-flow threshold at which the hydrodynamic load should initiate motion towards closure. The application developed in this chapter converts that threshold into a comparison of the axial loads predicted for the four investigated obturators. It combines the audited integral responses with the local-velocity diagnostics of Chapter~\ref{ch:results}, and presents the calculations through a \texttt{Streamlit} interface with interactive \texttt{Plotly} graphs \cite{streamlit_docs,plotly_docs}.

\section{Aim, Inputs and Mechanical Interpretation}
\label{sec:recommendation_logic}

The interface requires two design inputs: the working gas, hydrogen or nitrogen, and the desired threshold mass flow. The latter is entered in $\mathrm{g\,s^{-1}}$ and converted internally to $\mathrm{kg\,s^{-1}}$. All four obturators are evaluated together. The principal output is the fitted axial hydrodynamic force at the requested flow, accompanied by the pressure loss, the estimated local Mach number and an assessment of whether the operating point lies within the available CFD coverage.

The force supplies one term in the mechanical balance introduced in Chapter~\ref{ch:efv_operation}. At incipient closing motion, a preliminary balance at a specified reference position $x_0$ can be expressed as
\begin{equation}
F_{\mathrm{fluid,close}}(x_0,\dot m_{\mathrm{thr}})
\simeq F_0+kx_0+F_{\mathrm{friction}}+F_{\mathrm{other}},
\label{eq:tool_spring_balance}
\end{equation}
where $F_0$ is the spring preload and $k$ is its stiffness. The application's recorded force is the positive CFD $x$-component, including both pressure and viscous contributions. Its projection onto the actual closing direction must be verified before applying Eq.~\eqref{eq:tool_spring_balance}.

A single threshold force does not determine $k$: preload, available stroke, friction and the other mechanical loads are also required. Moreover, the CFD campaign contains one fixed obturator position, so it does not provide the displacement-dependent fluid load during closure. The tool therefore supports selection of a preliminary opposing spring load and comparison of geometries; it does not calculate a unique spring stiffness, a closure time or a certified trip setting. The geometry with the greatest force is not automatically preferred, because its pressure penalty can also be greater.

\section{Reference Dataset and Mass-Flow Conversion}
\label{sec:tool_dataset_priority}
\label{sec:tool_430bar}

The data bundle contains the 24 audited high-pressure response records: two gases, four obturator geometries and three inlet velocities, $5$, $10$ and $15\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. Hydrogen is the default gas, while nitrogen provides the secondary industrial-gas comparison. The reference state is fixed at $437\,\mathrm{bar}$ absolute and $233\,\mathrm{K}$. Temperature and pressure are displayed rather than offered as adjustable inputs, since the present dataset does not support predictions at arbitrary thermodynamic states.

The fitted responses are the terminal mean axial force and inlet-to-outlet pressure loss. Most means use iterations 2700--3000; nitrogen A at $5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ uses the shorter interval 1072--1372. This distinction and the terminal coefficients of variation (CVs) are retained in the application. OpenFOAM force outputs already include the prescribed reference density; no additional density multiplication is applied to them. The pressure losses in the audited table are already expressed in kPa.

For each selected gas, the input mass flow is converted using
\begin{equation}
\dot m=\rho_{\mathrm{ref}}A_{\mathrm{in,mesh}}U_{\mathrm{in}},
\qquad
U_{\mathrm{in}}=\frac{\dot m}{\rho_{\mathrm{ref}}A_{\mathrm{in,mesh}}},
\label{eq:tool_mass_conversion}
\end{equation}
with $A_{\mathrm{in,mesh}}=4.658925\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m^2}$. This is the discretized inlet area used for actual flow reporting in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}; the nominal CAD inlet area used in the force-coefficient normalization is not substituted into this conversion. The prescribed densities are $33.77\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ for hydrogen and $481.352\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ for nitrogen. The corresponding sampled mass-flow intervals are approximately $7.87$--$23.60\,\mathrm{g\,s^{-1}}$ and $112.13$--$336.39\,\mathrm{g\,s^{-1}}$, respectively.

Each gas has its own fitted curves. Nitrogen forces are not obtained by scaling a hydrogen curve by density: the two simulations also have different viscosities and Reynolds numbers. Similarly, the four geometries remain discrete candidates; the tool does not interpolate between hole counts or diameters. Ambient-reference simulations are excluded from this high-pressure model.

\section{Quadratic Response Fits}
\label{sec:selection_coverage}

At a fixed gas state and geometry, a nearly constant force coefficient gives $F_x\propto\rho U_{\mathrm{in}}^2$. This motivates the one-parameter parabola
\begin{equation}
\widehat F_x(U)=k_F U^2,
\qquad
k_F=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{3}U_i^2\overline F_{x,i}}
{\sum_{i=1}^{3}U_i^4},
\label{eq:tool_quadratic_fit}
\end{equation}
which minimizes $\sum_i(\overline F_{x,i}-k_FU_i^2)^2$. A separate coefficient is calculated for each gas--geometry pair, with equal weights on the three terminal means. Constraining the curve through the origin expresses zero flow-induced load at zero flow within the present model. A free three-coefficient quadratic could pass exactly through three points, but would have no remaining residual information and could predict a non-zero flow-induced load at zero flow.

Using Eq.~\eqref{eq:tool_mass_conversion}, the same curve can be written directly in the designer's input variable:
\begin{equation}
\widehat F_x(\dot m)=
\frac{k_F}{(\rho_{\mathrm{ref}}A_{\mathrm{in,mesh}})^2}\dot m^2.
\label{eq:tool_mass_parabola}
\end{equation}
Thus a specified mass flow selects one point on each geometry's parabola. This is a regression-based interpolation within the sampled interval, not an exact interpolant through every CFD mean. The independent pressure-loss fit uses the same form, $\widehat{\Delta p}=k_pU^2$, to expose the hydraulic penalty associated with the selected force.

\section[Local Mach Screening]{Local Mach Screening and Additional CFD Requirements}
\label{sec:tool_mach_screen}

The inlet Mach number alone misses acceleration through the obturator passages. The application therefore uses the full-volume cell-centred velocity maxima from Section~\ref{sec:near_wall_verification}, rather than the lower peaks sampled by central contour planes. For each gas and obturator, an observed amplification envelope is defined by
\begin{equation}
\alpha=\max_{i=1,2,3}
\left(\frac{U_{\max,\mathrm{3D},i}}{U_{\mathrm{in},i}}\right),
\qquad
\widehat{Ma}(\dot m)=
\frac{\alpha\dot m}{a_{\mathrm{ref}}\rho_{\mathrm{ref}}A_{\mathrm{in,mesh}}}.
\label{eq:tool_mach_envelope}
\end{equation}
This construction bounds the three observed amplification ratios. Extending it to other flow rates assumes that the amplification remains comparable; it is not a guaranteed bound on unsimulated flow. The reference sound speeds are $1611.78\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ for hydrogen and $593.37\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ for nitrogen, evaluated at the stated thermodynamic reference condition as documented in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}. Nitrogen A's $5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ velocity maximum comes from iteration 1000, its latest complete saved volume; the remaining volumes are from iteration 3000. These snapshots are distinct from the integral averaging windows.

Solving the screening relation for $\widehat{Ma}=0.3$ gives
\begin{equation}
\dot m_{0.3}=\frac{0.3\,a_{\mathrm{ref}}\rho_{\mathrm{ref}}A_{\mathrm{in,mesh}}}{\alpha}.
\label{eq:tool_mach_flow_limit}
\end{equation}
The corresponding flow is drawn as a labelled vertical line on every force plot. Table~\ref{tab:tool_mach_limits} lists the values used by the application. These are estimated screening boundaries, rather than CFD-measured transition points. In particular, the distant hydrogen boundaries require extrapolating the observed amplification well beyond the sampled mass-flow range.

Nitrogen A illustrates why the marker must be interpreted carefully. Its largest observed amplification ratio occurs at $5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. Projecting that ratio gives a boundary of $335.09\,\mathrm{g\,s^{-1}}$, slightly below the highest sampled flow of $336.39\,\mathrm{g\,s^{-1}}$. The actual full-volume maximum at the latter CFD point corresponds to $Ma=0.2979$. The more restrictive envelope does not contradict that value: it is a deliberately cautious projection based on a different sampled amplification ratio.

Two separate checks govern the outputs. A point outside the sampled flow interval is labelled as an unvalidated extrapolation even when its estimated Mach number is below 0.3. A point at or above the projected Mach boundary is flagged as requiring further CFD with an appropriate compressibility assessment; the numerical force and pressure design outputs are withheld. A dotted mathematical extension remains on the plot to locate the requested point, but is explicitly labelled as a formal projection. Mach 0.3 is a screening criterion, not a physical discontinuity at which a real force ceases to exist. Below it, changes in density, pressure, temperature, flow structure and obturator position can still limit the model, particularly for the largest nitrogen pressure losses.

\section{Presentation of Results and Worked Examples}
\label{sec:tool_outputs}

The results table places the four geometries side by side in a fixed A--D order. It reports fitted axial force, pressure loss, estimated local Mach number, projected boundary flow and assessment status. No automatic ranking is imposed. This allows the designer to compare the opposing requirements of obtaining sufficient fluid load and limiting normal-flow loss.

Each geometry has its own interactive force plot. The original CFD means remain visible, with temporal standard-deviation bars, while an orange diamond marks the selected threshold. Solid curves and blue shading identify the sampled interval; dashed curves indicate extrapolation. Red shading begins at the projected Mach boundary, and a red cross replaces the selected marker when it represents only a formal extension beyond that boundary. Separate views of the full screening range and the CFD/selected-flow range prevent a distant boundary from making the original data unreadable. Hover information and zoom allow individual points to be inspected without altering the calculation.

At a hydrogen threshold of $15\,\mathrm{g\,s^{-1}}$, the equivalent inlet velocity is approximately $9.534\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. The fitted axial forces for A, B, C and D are approximately $20.249$, $1.920$, $5.371$ and $0.617\,\mathrm{N}$, respectively. All four points lie within the sampled interval and below their Mach screening boundaries. Figure~\ref{fig:tool_hydrogen_example} shows this comparison over the sampled flow interval. It makes the geometry-dependent load differences visible while retaining the CFD evidence supporting the curves.

The downloadable comparison retains inputs, units, assessment states, source revision and numerical parameters. When the Mach screen is exceeded, the design-force field is empty and the formal projection is stored separately with its qualification. This preserves the meaning of the result when it is transferred to a design worksheet.

\section{Implementation and Portability with Streamlit}
\label{sec:software_architecture}

The implementation separates numerical calculations, plotting and interface presentation. The numerical layer loads the audited tables, converts mass flow, computes the fitted coefficients and applies the coverage and Mach checks. The interface then displays those results without loading any CFD mesh. This small data requirement makes repeated evaluations immediate and avoids requiring an OpenFOAM or ParaView installation on the designer's computer.

\texttt{Streamlit} was selected because it exposes Python calculations through standard browser controls and updates the results when an input changes. \texttt{Plotly} supplies interactive curves, hover values and zoom \cite{streamlit_docs,plotly_docs}. The same application can be launched locally with \texttt{python -m streamlit run streamlit\_app.py} or hosted on a compatible server. A hosted instance makes the interface accessible from other devices through a browser while that server is available. Local operation remains possible without a network connection after the dependencies have been installed.

The deliverable is an executable Python web application, rather than a standalone compiled Windows executable. A Windows launcher creates an isolated Python environment and starts the application; documented commands provide the equivalent workflow on macOS and Linux. Pinned dependencies and bundled response data make the application reproducible, although a compatible Python runtime is still required for local execution. The tested environment uses Python 3.13, Streamlit 1.66.1 and Plotly 7.1.0. No hosted deployment is assumed by the calculations or required to reproduce the thesis examples.

\section{Verification, Traceability and Intended Use}
\label{sec:industrial_traceability}

The data bundle records the source repository revision and cryptographic hashes of its audited input files. Integrity checks at launch prevent silent changes to the underlying means or velocity maxima. The short nitrogen averaging interval and the saved-volume iterations remain available in the provenance view. Replacing the CFD campaign requires reviewing the state, units, geometry and averaging convention before regenerating the bundle; adding a new response is not equivalent to validating a new operating range.

Automated checks cover the analytic quadratic fit, mass-flow conversion, all eight gas--geometry Mach boundaries, the exact-boundary withholding rule, extrapolation, zero and invalid inputs, short-run provenance, changed-data detection and exported-result semantics. Streamlit interaction tests exercise gas switching, threshold changes, the four plotted geometries and the Mach warning. The interface is also inspected in a browser, and the thesis illustrations are generated from the same numerical model as the application.

The resulting tool connects a required threshold mass flow to an auditable estimate of fixed-position hydrodynamic load. Its contribution is a practical and transparent preliminary design comparison. Final spring selection and valve qualification additionally require the mechanical force balance, displacement-dependent loading and appropriate validation of the operating conditions, consistent with the EFV design scope established in Chapter~\ref{ch:efv_operation}.
