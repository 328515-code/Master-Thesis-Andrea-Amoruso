\chapter{Results and Post-Processing}
\label{ch:results}

The post-processing procedure is designed for the complete CFD parameter matrix, comprising two gases, three inlet velocities and four obturator geometries. The hydrogen results presented in this chapter use the ambient-reference property values of Table~\ref{tab:fluid_properties}, so the reported dimensional forces and pressure differences represent that baseline, \emph{not} the selected $430\,\mathrm{bar}$, $-40\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ dispensing condition. The hydrogen results are used here as a worked engineering comparison of pressure drop and axial hydrodynamic force. The emphasis is on consistent response extraction, dimensional interpretation and the consequences of obturator geometry for excess-flow-valve design.

\section{Structure of OpenFOAM Post-Processing Data}
\label{sec:postprocessing_structure}

The case \texttt{controlDict} selects the steady-state solver \texttt{simpleFoam} and specifies an end index of 3000. The output index labels solver iterations, not physical seconds. The analysis employs the following post-processing data streams:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Area-averaged kinematic pressure}, for the inlet and outlet, in \texttt{inlet\_pressure\_avg/0/surfaceFieldValue.dat} and \texttt{outlet\_pressure\_avg/0/surfaceFieldValue.dat}.
\item \textbf{Integrated inlet velocity}, in \texttt{inlet\_volume\_flux/0/surfaceFieldValue.dat}. Its axial component is the inlet volumetric flow rate in $\mathrm{m^3\,s^{-1}}$.
\item \textbf{Near-wall distance}, in \texttt{yPlus1/0/yPlus.dat}, reporting minimum, maximum and average $y^+$ for the solid patches at intervals of 100 iterations.
\item \textbf{Pressure and viscous forces}, in \texttt{forceCoeffs\_Obt/0/forces.dat}. Despite the output-directory name, the actual function-object type is \texttt{forces}; its values are dimensional forces in newtons.
\end{itemize}

For each case the dimensional inlet-to-outlet pressure difference and streamwise obturator force are determined using
\begin{equation}
\Delta p=\rho_{\rm ref}(P_{\rm in}-P_{\rm out}),\qquad
F_x=F_{p,x}+F_{\nu,x},
\label{eq:processed_responses}
\end{equation}
where $P$ is kinematic pressure and $\rho_{\rm ref}=0.0838\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ for the hydrogen cases. Because the function object already uses \texttt{rhoInf = 0.0838}, $F_x$ is \emph{not multiplied by density again}. Positive $F_x$ denotes the global $+x$ direction; it is not necessarily the valve's mechanical closing direction.

\section{Data Extraction and Preliminary Stability Screening}
\label{sec:plotly_dashboard}

The post-processing workflow creates standalone HTML/Plotly reports for the analyzed cases together with a \texttt{Master\_Dashboard.html} navigation page. In addition, their raw \texttt{.dat} histories can be parsed directly. For a consistent preliminary assessment, each response was averaged over the final 301 records, covering solver iterations 2700--3000 inclusive:
\begin{equation}
\bar R=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N R_i,\qquad
{\rm CV}(R)=\frac{\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N(R_i-\bar R)^2}}{|\bar R|},\quad N=301.
\label{eq:terminal_average}
\end{equation}
Here CV is the relative standard deviation within the averaging window. It is a descriptive stability indicator, not an independent convergence or uncertainty estimate.

All twelve hydrogen cases have finite force and pressure histories ending at iteration 3000. The terminal-window CV ranges from approximately $0.33\%$ to $3.58\%$ for $F_x$ and from $0.16\%$ to $2.25\%$ for $\Delta p$. The largest values occur for obturator B at $10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. These coefficients describe fluctuation within the selected averaging window; they are useful for consistent numerical screening rather than a replacement for the convergence and mesh checks outlined in Chapter~\ref{ch:numerical_setup}.

The inlet patch area reported in the hydrogen surface-integration files is $4.616760\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m^2}$, approximately $3.38\%$ smaller than the nominal inlet CAD area of $4.778\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m^2}$. The recorded inlet volume fluxes at 5, 10 and $15\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ are approximately $2.30838\times10^{-4}$, $4.61676\times10^{-4}$ and $6.92514\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^3\,s^{-1}}$, respectively. Inlet flow-rate checks should therefore use the \emph{actual discretized area}. The integrated inlet area is therefore used consistently when comparing prescribed velocity and computed flow rate. Global continuity is assessed separately through inlet and outlet face fluxes in the numerical solution.

For each geometry, the preliminary pressure difference and $+x$ force increase with inlet velocity. At each tested velocity, both response magnitudes follow the order A, C, B, D, from highest to lowest. This ordering agrees qualitatively with the reduction in nominal open area described in Chapter~\ref{ch:doe}, but local flow structure and surface-pressure distribution also influence the integrated responses.

At $15\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, obturator A produces approximately $980\,\mathrm{Pa}$ pressure drop and $0.1634\,\mathrm{N}$ axial force, compared with approximately $52.5\,\mathrm{Pa}$ and $0.00412\,\mathrm{N}$ for obturator D. The strong difference highlights a pressure-loss versus hydrodynamic-load trade-off. It does not establish a preferred valve without an independently specified force balance, sign convention and pressure-loss requirement. An exact quadratic velocity law has not been assumed or fitted from these preliminary data.

\section{Near-Wall Diagnostics and Physical Interpretation}
\label{sec:near_wall_verification}

At solver iteration 3000, the maximum reported obturator-patch $y^+$ for geometry A is approximately $0.53$, $1.76$ and $2.92$ at the three increasing velocities. These values and the low $y^+$ found on other geometries should be interpreted together with the realized near-wall mesh, surface-layer survival and turbulence-wall treatment. Small $y^+$ alone does not demonstrate a reliable mesh.

The wall-distance distribution is considered together with the realized boundary-layer mesh and the assumptions of the turbulence closure (Section~\ref{sec:numerical_verification}). The numerical results are evaluated for the reference-state properties defined in Chapter~\ref{ch:doe}; application to the broader pressure and temperature envelope of hydrogen refuelling requires a consistent thermodynamic description, as explained in Chapter~\ref{ch:efv_operation}.


\section{Comparison Between Reference-State and Dispensing Conditions}
\label{sec:results_operating_states}

The ambient-reference CFD data offer a controlled way to examine the influence of hole geometry and inlet velocity, while the application of the EFV downstream of the pre-cooler motivates a distinct high-pressure, low-temperature analysis. To interpret the latter, the relevant densities and kinematic viscosities must be evaluated consistently at $430\,\mathrm{bar}$ absolute and $-40\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$, rather than using the ambient values from Table~\ref{tab:fluid_properties} \cite{nist_refprop_online}. Both sets of simulations can be analysed using the same pressure and force definitions, but the results should be labelled by thermodynamic state and must not be combined as if they represented equal mass-flow rates.

In particular, dimensional pressure drop is obtained by multiplying the computed \emph{kinematic} pressure difference by the reference density for the corresponding case, and the \texttt{forces} function object already applies \texttt{rhoInf} to obtain the load in newtons. The two states may also have different Reynolds numbers at the same imposed inlet velocity. Interpretation within a refuelling protocol additionally requires attention to the variable delivery pressure and temperature rather than assuming one design point describes the entire fill \cite{sae_j2601,elgowainy2017}.

\section{Implications for Excess-Flow Valve Design}
\label{sec:results_efv_implications}

Pressure loss and axial hydrodynamic force must be interpreted together. For normal refuelling service, the pressure loss is part of the required system operating margin. The force acting on the obturator is one input to the potential closing motion of a spring-loaded EFV. A geometry that maximizes force is not necessarily suitable if it also produces an unacceptable restriction during intended flow \cite{white2016,crane410}.

The hydrogen response tables illustrate this trade-off. At $15\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, the computed pressure difference for obturator A is about $18.7$ times the value for obturator D, while its axial force is about $39.7$ times larger. The unequal ratios show that force cannot be obtained simply by multiplying the overall pressure loss by a single constant effective area. The three-dimensional distribution of surface pressure and shear contributes to the net load.

For company-facing engineering comparison, a useful result presentation places $\Delta p(U_{\mathrm{in}})$ and $F_x(U_{\mathrm{in}})$ side by side, with explicit reference-state density and a consistent force-axis definition. The dimensionless responses and factorial contrasts derived in Chapter~\ref{ch:doe} then help distinguish effects of velocity, passage area and hole distribution. Closing performance also depends on the mechanical force balance established in Section~\ref{sec:efv_principle}; the reported steady fixed-obturator force is an input to that balance, not itself a dynamic trip prediction.
